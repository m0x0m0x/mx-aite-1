# Slice B — Governance Reality vs Governance Marketing, and Treasury / Organizational Integrity

Scope note: token economics, regulatory licensing, smart-contract code review, and case studies are out of scope (owned by other slices). Everything below concerns *organizational and on-chain control reality*. All sources accessed 2026-10-06.

## B1 The DAO-claims gap: why "a multisig exists" ≠ "governed safely"

### Findings

A multisig is a **signature-collection mechanism**, not a governance property. It carries no intrinsic constraint on who the signers are, how independent they are, how much notice observers get, or which contracts the multisig can actually reach. Five things must separately be true, and each can fail while the project still truthfully says "we have a multisig":

**(a) Threshold vs. number of signers.** The only security-relevant quantity is `M` in an `M-of-N` scheme, and what `N` counts. SEAL's baseline guidance is minimum 3 signers, ≥50% threshold, and **7+ signers for multisigs holding $1M+**, with hardware wallets mandatory and `N-of-N` explicitly to be avoided because losing one key then permanently bricks the wallet [B9]. This is exactly the shape of the failures documented in B6: Harmony's bridge required only **two of four** validator keys [B34]; a 2-of-2 proxy-admin pair halves the independence property entirely.

**(b) Signer independence, specifically from the founder/team.** Count of signers is not enough if they are employees of one company, one jurisdiction, or one founder's payroll. SEAL requires *strategic signer distribution*: different hardware-wallet manufacturers, geographic separation, keys assigned to different trusted individuals, diverse client software, and role diversity across executives/developers/operations [B9]. Radiant Capital's signers were "geographically distributed" and used hardware wallets and still lost ~$50M because three *devices* were compromised — i.e. independence of people does not survive compromised endpoints [B33].

**(c) Timelock delay duration, and whether the timelock is real.** A timelock's only function is to create *notice*, so that exit and challenge are possible. SEAL's operational principle is "build in slowness by design: speed is a risk, forced urgency is one of the primary tools attackers use in social-engineering and key-compromise scenarios" [B9]. Compound's design is a documented reference point: 25,000 COMP proposal threshold, 2-day voting delay, 3-day voting period, 400,000-COMP quorum (4% of COMP), then a **2-day** Timelock — "in total, any change to the protocol takes at least one week" [B2], with the delay hard-coded as the minimum possible notice [B2].

**(d) Whether the timelock actually holds admin over the contracts that matter.** This is the single most-overlooked check. Compound's Comptroller/cTokens are admined by the Timelock [B2]. OP Stack publishes the actual chain: the L1 Proxy Admin is owned by a **2-of-2** Safe whose two owners are the Optimism Foundation 5/7 multisig and the Security Council multisig; the System Config Owner is a 5-of-7; the Batcher is "typically a hot wallet" [B4]. Uniswap publishes its Timelock (`0x1a9C8182C09F50C8318d769245beA52c32BE35BC`) and GovernorBravo (`0x408ED635...`) addresses so the chain of custody is auditable [B10]. A timelock that exists but does not own the impl, the factory, the pause authority, or the treasury is decorative.

**(e) Emergency-pause power and its scope.** Pause powers are the sharpest governance-vs-marketing gap. Best practice constrains them to the *minimum necessary action*: SEAL states an emergency path "should only be able to pause the protocol, not upgrade contracts or move funds… The more powerful an action, the more restrictions and delays it should require" [B9]. OP Stack's Guardian role is scoped exactly this way — `OptimismPortal`'s privileged `GUARDIAN` "can pause withdrawals", preventing L2→L1 execution, and nothing more [B40]. Arbitrum's Security Council holds *real* chain-owner powers — per the Amended Constitution, chain owners "have the power to take administrative actions that change a chain's core protocol and code and/or alter any of its core parameters," and owner privileges on Arbitrum One and Nova were given to **both** the DAO and the Foundation's Security Council [B7]. Arbitrum constrains it by election and removal rules: 12 members, DAO-elected, max 3 from one organization, removable by 10% of votable tokens voting with ≥5/6 in favor or by 9 Council members [B6]. Optimism's Charter is a 75%-threshold Gnosis Safe whose stated goal is to hand admin keys to actors "accountable to Optimism Governance", with the explicit principle that "a successful Security Council is one whose role is progressively and programmatically reduced over time" [B5].

**Verifiable checks (all mechanical):**
1. On the target contracts, read the `owner()` / `admin()` / role-admin getters. Confirm the returned address is the timelock, not an EOA or a different Safe. Recurse until you reach a contract with no further admin (or a documented pause-only role).
2. Call `delay()` / `minDelay()` on the timelock. Convert to wall-clock. Flag <48h as weak; ≥7 days is materially better notice.
3. Read `threshold()` and `getOwners()` / `getThreshold()` on every Safe in the chain. Compute M/N. Flag N<3, threshold<50%, N-of-N, or N<7 for a >$1M wallet.
4. Enumerate each owner's address across the org chart. Flag signers that are (i) employees of one legal entity, (ii) funded by one grant/vesting contract, (iii) EOAs rather than hardware-wallet-derived addresses with public attestation.
5. Simulate a parameter change: can it be executed without passing the timelock? Test by checking whether the parameter's setter has `onlyOwner` pointing at the timelock or at something else.
6. For every emergency role found, enumerate the reachable function set. If it includes upgrade, mint, or treasury-transfer functions, that is a governance-integrity finding, not a pause mechanism.
7. Confirm the quorum/threshold/voting-delay parameters are themselves governed through the same timelock (Compound: `_setProposalThreshold` is `admin`-only with MIN/MAX constants baked in [B1]).
8. Check whether the emergency path has a bounded expiry/recovery and whether the multisig has to be re-approving itself.

## B2 Governance participation reality: delegation concentration, quorum gaming, plutocracy

### Findings

Voting-power distribution is the load-bearing legitimacy metric, and the published evidence is damning and reproducible.

**Delegate concentration.** Kani et al. (ETH Zurich), using complete holder/delegate/proposal/vote data for Compound, Uniswap and ENS, found voting-rights Gini coefficients "around 0.99" for Compound and Uniswap — higher than the Gini of national wealth distributions cited in the same paper (US 0.850, Europe 0.814) [B11]. Their Nakamoto coefficients — the minimum number of delegates holding >50% of voting power — were **8 for Compound, 11 for Uniswap, 18 for ENS** [B11]. A larger, later ETH Zurich study of 21 DAOs (on-chain governance across lending, DEXs, infrastructure and common-goods funding) found that **17 of 21 governance systems could have a majority voting power controlled by fewer than 10 addresses**, and "for half of the analyzed DAOs, the Nakamoto coefficient is even no larger than 3" [B12].

**Single-holder delegates vs community delegates.** The distinction that matters for legitimacy is whether a delegate represents many holders or one. Kani et al. define a *single holder delegate* as one receiving >50% of its tokens from one holder; a *community delegate* receives less than 50% from any single holder. For ENS, Gitcoin and Hop ~half of all votes sat with community delegates; for **Compound, Fei and Uniswap the community-delegate vote share was ~10% or less** [B11]. "Most voting power is held by delegates mainly representing a single token holder… there is little evidence of a substantial community-participation in the decision-making" [B12].

**Delegation amplifies concentration, it does not disperse it.** A 2026 *Frontiers in Blockchain* audit of 52 token protocols computing HHI after excluding protocol-controlled addresses found that **delegation amplified voting power above token holdings in 13 of 18** sampled protocols, with 5 design-driven dispersion exceptions (ENS 0.48×, GMX 0.87×, JUP 0.12×, LPT 0.27×, HNT 0.26–0.39×) [B14]. This is the mechanism-level version of "delegated voting power is not decentralization": it converts *N* independent holders into *M* correlated decision-makers, and the decision about who counts as a holder is itself captured.

**Participation is thin and top-heavy.** a16z researchers analysed 18 Ethereum DAOs from Jan 2021–Dec 2023, ~250,000 voters and 1,700 proposals, and found **only ~17% of voting power was delegated to others**, and that delegates participated on only **33% of proposal votes** on average; medium-small holders delegate most often; and voters who had voted before were more likely to delegate [B13]. Note the tension with [B11]: very little of the supply votes *at all*, and what does vote is largely self-delegation (a holder delegating to itself to be able to vote). The ETH Zurich dataset makes the cost side explicit: for Uniswap, **88% of votes cast had voting power below 10 tokens and 47% below 1 token**, while 2.5M tokens were needed to submit a proposal and 40M to pass one — i.e. the proposal right is priced ~4 orders of magnitude above the median vote [B12].

**Quorum design and gaming.** Quorum is the gameable dial. Governor Bravo's defeat condition is `forVotes <= againstVotes || forVotes < quorumVotes` [B1] — a *fixed absolute* quorum means that if participation falls, proposals must be smaller/less contentious to clear the bar, or the quorum must be lowered, which is itself a governance decision. Optimum-like systems have the opposite failure: a quorum measured as a fraction of *participating* votes can be cleared by a single large voter. Arbitrum's DVP-Quorum thread is an explicit design discussion of this [B38]. Cross-Jurisdiction analysis (Erasmus/RSM) finds delegation in Uniswap is partly *merit/reputation*-based — a genuine positive — but also finds holders delegate disproportionately to addresses affiliated with the controlling VC (a16z), and characterise this as possible "window-dressing" around decentralization [B17]. This is the sharpest academic statement of the plutocracy signal: an airdropped token plus delegation-based governance where a VC-affiliated class receives outsized delegations is a marketing surface, not a control surface.

**Proposal spam / ceremonial governance.** DeFiLlama's governance dashboard is a Tier-C check that anyone can run in seconds: Network Nervous System shows **19,505 proposals with 18,453 successful (94.6%)**; WaterNeuron 9,472/6,893; CLever 1,585/1,046 [B15]. Near-100% success rates across thousands of proposals, with daily cadence, are the signature of a *ratification* process (a program upgrade voting on every instruction), not deliberation. Also on that dashboard, projects with 80–400 proposals and ~85–99% success (Gold DAO 393/382, BOOM DAO 735/656, Stargate 81/81) should be read as operational plumbing. ETH Zurich's "pointless governance transactions" analysis quantifies the immature-UX layer: at ENS ~30% of all daily votes were cast by addresses holding zero voting power, and the share *rose* over time; most DAOs had >10% useless votes/delegations, many >20% [B12].

**Why "delegated voting power" damages legitimacy.** Delegation is a legitimate design (it is the mechanism that lets passive holders be represented), but three properties make it illegitimate in practice: (i) delegation is revocable but effectively irrevocable — an absent holder can never notice or exit; (ii) the delegate set is itself concentrated, so delegation converts a broad nominal right into a narrow actual one — measurable as `voting HHI / holding HHI > 1.0`, which is true in 13/18 protocols [B14]; (iii) self-delegation by the incumbent means "delegate count" overstates pluralism.

**Verifiable checks:**
1. Compute Nakamoto coefficient over *delegates* (min addresses to exceed 50% of `getVotes`-equivalent voting power). Flag >10 as concentrated; >20 as plutocratic.
2. Compute Gini of delegated voting power and of raw balances. Note the paper's ordering: delegate distribution can be *more* equal than holder distribution [B11] — a trap for naive scoring.
3. For each top-10 delegate, compute the share of its voting power sourced from its single largest delegator. If >50%, it is a single-holder delegate, not a representative [B11].
4. Compute voting-HHI ÷ holding-HHI after excluding protocol-controlled addresses and exchange-custody wallets. >1.0 = delegation concentrates power [B14].
5. Over a trailing 12 months: count proposals, count executed proposals, compute `executed / created`. >0.9 with >50 proposals = ceremonial governance.
6. Compute median voting power per `VoteCast` event; compare to `proposalThreshold` and quorum. Very low median vote weight = rubber-stamping.
7. Compute share of supply that has ever delegated at all, and share of total supply that has ever voted. Both being single-digit percent invalidates "token-holder governance".
8. Read quorum as a *fraction of total supply* vs a *fraction of cast votes*, and check whether quorum is governable — if yes, incumbents can lower the bar.
9. Check for snapshot-vs-onchain divergence (off-chain Snapshot votes binding without on-chain execution path). Off-chain votes with no enforceable on-chain consequence are not governance rights.

## B3 Treasury management: credible policy vs "a big illiquid bag"

### Findings

**The disclosed-address baseline is now industry-standard and cheap.** DeFiLlama publishes a "Net Treasury Rankings — Excl. Native Token" table explicitly separating spendable assets from the project's own token [B23], and defines Treasury in its glossary as "the value of coins held in ownership by the protocol. By default this excludes coins created by the protocol itself" [B16]. That single definitional choice is the whole ballgame: a treasury number that silently includes the native token is a *price chart*, not a balance sheet.

**The "big illiquid bag" failure mode is documented and large.** Keyrock's analysis of 25 crypto-native treasuries with >$5.6bn aggregate holdings reports: **~86% of aggregate treasury value in native governance tokens; median stablecoin allocation just 3.6%; median protocol allocates only 7% of treasury to income-bearing positions; 93% ($5.2bn) sits idle**; and treasuries "spent 57.4% of observed time more than 50% below prior highs" [B19]. The failure mode is therefore three-layered: (1) the treasury is denominated in the asset it is trying to survive without, so a 80% drawdown is simultaneously a solvency event; (2) because there is no stable buffer, the only funding source in a bear market is *selling governance tokens into the weakness* — i.e. the DAO is forced to sell the asset whose price its own token depends on; (3) with 7% productive allocation there is no revenue stream, so runway is a pure function of token price. Keyrock's counter-example is instructive: Aave reduced native-token concentration to 41% with a 33% stablecoin buffer, and describes this as the mechanism that converts a treasury from a price chart into a funding source — even 10–20% in stablecoins/ETH/BTC at 3–8% yields "sufficient to meaningfully extend runway" [B19].

**The academic cross-section agrees.** Schellinger, Fiedler & Steinmetz (Blockchain Research Lab), analysing the 20 largest DAO treasuries ($9.6bn combined market cap), find **81.67% of treasury assets invested in native DAO tokens**, describing the resulting exposure to volatility as "alarming," and note "most of these DAOs exhibit negative performance metrics" [B20]. Two independent methods, same order of magnitude.

**Treasury-to-market-cap ratio.** This ratio is the leverage/priority claim on your own token. A treasury holding 80% of a token's market cap in that same token has a *circular* balance sheet: the "assets" line is a claim on itself. Scoring should compute `treasury_value / token_market_cap` and, separately, `treasury_ex_native_token / token_market_cap` (DeFiLlama publishes exactly the latter as a ranked list [B23]). A high native-inclusive ratio with a low net ratio is a treasury that cannot fund payroll without liquidating its own governance stake. Note also DeFiLlama's oFDV definition: outstanding supply = total supply minus supply "not yet allocated to anything (e.g. coins in treasury or reserve)" [B16] — i.e. tokens locked in a *vesting* contract may be counted as outstanding even though they are not circulating and are not spendable; using market cap rather than oFDV understates the overhang.

**What a credible published treasury policy contains** (assembled from the above, with each element traceable): disclosed multisig addresses + roles; a stated runway (months) with the burn rate and revenue assumption behind it; an allocation policy with a stablecoin/liquid floor; a published approval-to-spend limit per proposal; and on-chain verifiability of balances. The best-practice statement of the last point comes from Keyrock: "Governance legitimacy depends on verifiability… solutions must integrate onchain visibility, whether through escrowed collateral contracts, tokenised position receipts, cryptographic attestations, or proof-of-reserve style reconciliation" [B19].

**Runway disclosure is rare and therefore differentiating.** Two genuinely verifiable public examples: Uniswap Foundation posts quarterly and annual financial summaries with an explicit statement of runway and earmark — at 31 Dec 2024, "$29.8 million in USD and stables on hand and UNI 0.59 million"; "$21.82 million… allocated towards grants"; "The remaining $7.97 million was to be used to fund operations expenses through the end of 2025"; FY2024 operating expenses $5.79M; revenue $1.11M; and it explicitly labels the statement "**unaudited** summary financials" [B21]. The Ethereum Foundation publishes an annual report with named teams, grant disclosure, and a stated Conflicts of Interest Policy [B22]. The honest caveat: neither is audited, and both are self-reported. Uniswap's own report is the more auditable artifact because it names a specific number (16 staff), a specific revenue figure, and a specific runway date.

**Verifiable checks:**
1. Locate the treasury multisig/Safe address and confirm it is readable on-chain; confirm the signer set matches a published claim.
2. Compute `treasury_total`, `treasury_stablecoins`, `treasury_native_token`, and the ratio of each to token market cap and to circulating supply.
3. Flag `stablecoin_share < 10%` as a funding-risk signal [B19 benchmark: median 3.6%].
4. Check whether the treasury's native-token position is held in a *vesting* contract — if so it is neither spendable nor circulating.
5. Look for a published runway statement with a date and a burn number; if absent, that is itself the finding.
6. Diff treasury balances month-over-month and classify outflows (grants / payroll / ops / swaps). Swaps of native token into stables during a drawdown = forced-seller signal.
7. Confirm the treasury is governed by the same timelock/DAO as the protocol — a treasury controlled by a separate multisig is a single-signature upgrade path for all spending.
8. Check for a stated max-spend-per-proposal and compare it to the treasury size. A treasury with no per-proposal cap is governed by whoever holds enough tokens.

## B4 Transparency & disclosure: what serious projects publish, and does it correlate with outcomes

### Findings

**The IOSCO framing is the right frame.** IOSCO's 2023 DeFi report explicitly rejects the marketing claim as a legal category: "applicable regulatory frameworks apply to DeFi products, services, activities, and arrangements, **notwithstanding characterizations or assertions of decentralization** by persons or [others]" [B18]. It also supplies the "Big Picture" enterprise-level decomposition: the on-chain contracts are typically only one component of a larger enterprise involving (1) persons/entities engaged in capital formation, development and deployment — **founders, developers, foundations**; (2) investors; (3) those "involved in the ongoing activities in controlling and managing the enterprise, including those contributing to the ongoing operations of the enterprise (including those with control or sufficient influence over the governance of the enterprise)"; and (4) infrastructure/service providers [B18]. Applied to a legitimacy score: *the entity set behind the DAO is the thing to score, and it is usually not disclosed.*

**Documented correlation between disclosure and outcomes — yes, with nuance.** Moser, "Do whitepapers matter? Investigating the long-term effects of cryptocurrency whitepapers" (*Journal of Financial Markets/Finance Research Letters*, 2025), analysed **1,231 cryptocurrencies, 315 of which have whitepapers**, and found "significantly higher average excess returns for coins accompanied with whitepapers, particularly in the long term, rather than immediately after being listed" [B24]. Critically, the quality dimensions do *not* behave uniformly: **larger whitepapers negatively correlate with excess returns, readability shows no consistent effect, and whitepaper sentiment is negatively connected to long-term excess returns**. Read precisely: the *existence* of a disclosure artifact is a positive signal; verbosity and promotional tone are not. This is exactly the right calibration for a scorer — reward "a published, current, falsifiable document exists" and penalise marketing prose.

**Disclosure is legally load-bearing, not cosmetic.** The SEC's SafeMoon action turns directly on a *treasury/technical disclosure* lie rather than a securities-classification issue: the SEC alleged the founder "assured investors that funds were safely locked and could not be withdrawn by anyone, including the Defendants, while held in SafeMoon's liquidity pool," whereas "large portions of the liquidity pool were never locked" — and price fell ~50% when the public learned this on 20 April 2021 after a >55,000% run and >$5.7bn market cap [B26]. The CACU chief's framing is quotable for a legitimacy rubric: "Decentralized finance claims to deliver transparency and predictable outcomes, but unregistered offerings lack the disclosures and accountability that the law demands" [B26]. The Quantstamp administrative proceeding is the complementary case: an ICO that *did* publish offering materials but claimed a Reg D Rule 506(c)/Reg S exemption it did not qualify for, settled with $1,979,201 disgorgement, $494,314 prejudgment interest, $1M civil penalty, and an undertaking to **transfer all remaining QSP to a Fair Fund to be permanently disabled or destroyed** [B27]. Disclosure of *use of proceeds* is a checkable field; both cases show it being falsified.

**Post-mortem disclosure culture is observable in real projects, with real variance.** Radiant Capital published a named post-mortem naming the specific organizational failure — the compromise was invisible in the Safe UI and Tenderly simulations, confirmed by SEAL911 and Hypernative, during a "routine multi-signature emissions adjustment" — and published five named preventative recommendations [B33]. Nomad published a full root-cause analysis walking through `acceptableRoot()`, `confirmAt[bytes32(0)] == 1`, and the combination that made `acceptableRoot(bytes32(0))` always return true [B32]. Wormhole published a minute-by-minute timeline and, unusually, disclosed the governance mechanics used to fix itself: transfers were paused when "a superminority of six nodes stopped relaying (~37% of the voting power on the network)" [B35]. The scoreable distinction is not *whether an incident happened* but whether a named artifact exists, is reachable without paywall or NDA, identifies the organizational root cause (not just the code bug), and names accountable individuals/roles.

**What serious projects publish (checklist):** (1) a legal entity name, jurisdiction, and registration number, plus the link between that entity and the DAO; (2) named team with verifiable pseudonymous track record — consistent, attributable, multi-year public output — or real names; (3) a working contact channel and a security contact; (4) a roadmap with dated retrospectives (planned vs shipped vs abandoned); (5) published treasury addresses and periodic financial statements, with the audit status stated; (6) published incident history with post-mortems; (7) a published governance constitution/process with named signers.

**Verifiable checks:**
1. Resolve the legal entity; check the corporate registry (or IRS Form 990 for US non-profits, e.g. Uniswap Foundation appears on ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer as org 883087770 [B21]) for the entity that signs off on the DAO's spending.
2. Fetch the most recent financial statement; check whether it says "audited" or "unaudited" — record which. Absence of any statement is the finding.
3. Check for dated retrospectives: count roadmap items with a promised date vs a completed/abandoned date. Ratio of promises with a stated outcome.
4. Attempt to reach the security contact and the general contact; a dead security contact is a live risk signal, not a nit.
5. For any incident claimed in marketing, locate the primary post-mortem. If only a third-party summary exists, the project has no disclosure culture.
6. Test whether the treasury disclosure resolves to on-chain addresses (see B3 check 1). Non-resolvable "treasury" numbers are unverifiable.

## B5 Team & insider risk: allocation vs. retention, and unlock structures

### Findings

**The key insight from the 2026 governance-concentration audit is that *launch allocation is the wrong object*.** The Frontiers study of 52 protocols states the standard sharply: "Token allocation is a launch document. Governance concentration is a live institutional state"; it tests insider/team/investor allocation at launch, protocol maturity, float, and valuation ratios against steady-state HHI and finds them **each uninformative** (insider allocation Pearson r = 0.09, p = 0.55, N = 50) [B14]. So a published "team 20% with 4-year vest" is *not* the risk measure.

**What does correlate is current insider retention.** Same paper: "protocols with more insider wallets among top holders are more concentrated (Spearman rho = 0.44, p = 0.005, N = 39, surviving a non-insider HHI tautology check at rho = 0.54)" [B14]. Operationally: the risk metric is *how much governance-relevant token the insiders still hold today, net of protocol-controlled addresses*, not what they were allotted.

**Holder-list measurement is systematically inflated and must be corrected.** The same paper quantifies the correction: excluding protocol-controlled addresses (a five-class typology; 133 exclusions across 38 protocols, plus 64 Nansen-entity-labeled CEX deposit wallets across 21 protocols in a 2026 custody-completion audit) changed measured concentration by a **median factor of 2.3×, maximum ~18×** [B14]. Its operative conclusion for a scorer: "Holder-list concentration is meaningless until the unit of control is identified" and "inequality metrics cannot substitute for direct concentration measurement" (Gini vs HHI r = 0.52 — distinct properties) [B14]. Any score that reads a top-100 holder list and sums addresses is double-counting treasuries, vesting contracts, bridges, staking contracts and exchange deposit addresses.

**"Vesting schedule exists but tokens already unlocked."** This failure is definitional and must be checked on-chain, not in a PDF: read the vesting/release contract for each team allocation and compare `released` vs `total`. Uniswap's structure is a good structural reference: the UNI airdrop distributor (`0x090D4613...`) sits alongside four "Four year-long vesting contract" addresses vested to the protocol treasury over Sept 2020–Sept 2025 (`0x4750c438...`, `0xe3953d9d...`, `0x4b4e140d...`, `0x3d30b1ab...`) [B10]. Note the direction of that vest: it vests *to the treasury*, i.e. to the DAO — the opposite of a founder drip, and materially better for legitimacy. Score vesting by *recipient*, not just by schedule.

**Insider-wallet tracking is a live, published methodology.** Nansen's "Smart Money" methodology documents how it labels two classes — "Institutions, Funds, and Influential Whales" and "Top Performing Onchain Participants" — from on-chain activity, precisely because these entities hold outsized token positions and their activity is otherwise obscured [B30]. This is Tier-C infrastructure for the insider-retention metric in [B14]: a scored protocol should have a maintained, labelled insider address set, not a static vesting table.

**Legal record on undisclosed/misrepresented insider conduct.** The SEC's action against SafeMoon alleges the founders and executives withdrew "crypto assets worth more than $200 million from the project," misappropriated investor funds, and then used the misappropriated assets to buy SafeMoon to prop up the price, with wash trading to manufacture activity [B26]. That is the insider-risk endpoint: undisclosed or unconstrained insider liquidity plus no treasury disclosure. The SEC's Terraform/Do Kwon action (Press Release 2023-32) and its Al-Naji action (2024-91) are further Tier-A instances in the same family [B28][B29].

**Verifiable checks:**
1. Enumerate top holders *after* excluding: protocol-controlled addresses (treasury, vesting, distributor, staking, bridge, wrapper), exchange deposit addresses (label via Nansen/Arkham-class sources), and known bridges.
2. Compute `insider_retained_share = insider-labelled governance-token balance / total supply`, and its share of *voting power* (apply delegation).
3. For each vesting contract, read `released/total` and identify the beneficiary address; flag any beneficiary that is not the DAO/treasury.
4. Check whether any insider wallet is a member of, or funded by, a governance multisig signer.
5. Test whether insider tokens are freely transferable (no clawback/repurchase obligation in the token or vesting contract).
6. Correlate `insider_retained_share` with governance HHI — per [B14], high insider retention should co-occur with high concentration. Disagreement is a measurement error in the scoring inputs.

## B6 Organizational security: signer-key compromise as an organizational (not code) failure

### Findings

Five named incidents, each of which is an *organizational* failure that a code audit would have passed.

**1. Ronin / Axie Infinity, March 2022 — the 5-of-9 threshold that met its own quorum.** Sky Mavis's post-mortem reports the attacker obtained **five of nine validator private keys**, draining 173,600 ETH and 25.5M USDC in two forged withdrawals. Four keys came from compromising Sky Mavis infrastructure and validator nodes via an employee compromise; the **fifth signature was obtained by the attacker through an old Axie DAO allowlist on a gas-free RPC endpoint**, i.e. the fifth signature was a *process* failure — an unauthenticated allowlist acted as a signature oracle. Follow-on changes: more validators, stricter internal procedures, zero-trust controls, external forensics, redesigned bridge [B36]. This is the canonical demonstration that a threshold is only as strong as the authentication around each signature, and that the "governance" surface included an unaudited off-chain allowlist.

**2. Harmony Horizon Bridge, June 2022 — 2-of-4 keys, generated on a server.** Harmony's own incident summary: "The perpetrator(s) compromised **at least two out of four private keys** of the bridge validators"; decrypting the keys "would have required several operations from within a secure set of servers to generate keys on-the-fly performed on servers with privileged access (authorized roles)"; and the attacker "employed a phishing scheme to trick at least one software developer to install malicious software on their laptop" [B34]. Every element of the SEAL baseline was violated simultaneously: N=4 (<3 is the floor but 2-of-4 means a single insider compromises it), keys on servers rather than hardware wallets, and signer distribution over one internal cloud estate. Harmony's remediation — pausing the *unaffected* Bitcoin bridge, expanding the key set, swapping in a new set of multisig signers, forming a Security Operations team, adopting SSO/MFA/VPN/separation-of-duties — is a template for what organizational disclosure looks like [B34]. The FBI confirmed the theft as $100M attributed to Lazarus [B34b].

**3. Radiant Capital, October 2024 — the compliant-signing, process-correct multisig.** Radiant's post-mortem is the single best source for why *procedural* compliance is not a control. Three long-standing DAO contributors, hardware wallets, geographically distributed, followed "industry standard operating procedures (SOP)": each transaction simulated on Tenderly and individually reviewed by multiple developers at each signature stage; Safe and Tenderly front-ends showed no anomalies; SEAL911 and Hypernative confirmed the compromise was undetectable during manual review. The mechanism: the Safe front-end displayed **legitimate transaction data while malicious transactions were signed and executed in the background**, and the Safe UI showed an error prompting the signer to retry — which the attackers used to "collect multiple compromised signatures over several attempts, all while mimicking the appearance of routine transaction failures." Ledger devices showed valid-looking blind signatures. Result: ~$50M drained from core markets on Arbitrum and BSC, plus exploitation of open approvals [B33]. Radiant's own recommendation #1 — a governance layer that halts on *any* signer anomaly — is exactly the SEAL "constrain emergency powers / build in slowness" principle [B9][B33].

**4. Wormhole, February 2022 — the signature set that was real but the verification that wasn't.** Wormhole's report: "The network is operated by a decentralized group of nineteen Guardians who sign each transmitted message… a message is treated as authentic if ⅔+ of the Guardians have signed it," and "Upgrades to the protocol and contracts require a ⅔+ supermajority vote of Guardians." The attacker "exploited a signature verification vulnerability… and tricked it into minting 120,000 uncollateralized Wormhole-wrapped ETH" — i.e. the 19-of-19-style quorum and the governance process were real and functioning; the failure was that the on-chain verifier accepted a legitimately-signed guardian attestation for the wrong domain/context. Incident duration ~16 hours; detected by "a routine check" of outstanding funds by contributors [B35]. Two transferable lessons: (i) the guardian *set* is not the security boundary — the verification logic is; (ii) a 19-signer, ⅔-supermajority governance structure still funnels a whole protocol upgrade through 13 co-operating signatures, and the report shows a "superminority of six nodes (~37% of voting power)" could unilaterally halt the network — liveness is centralized even where safety is distributed.

**5. Nomad, August 2022 — the deploy-script failure.** Nomad's RCA: the `Replica` was initialized with a Merkle root of `bytes32(0)`, and because `confirmAt[bytes32(0)]` was set to `1` at initialization, `acceptableRoot(bytes32(0))` "always returns true for any valid block timestamp" [B32]. The code-level bug is trivial (a default-value collision), but the *organizational* failure is that a copy-paste-style initialization mistake reached mainnet carrying the bridge's full outstanding balance — i.e. no independent verification that the committed root matched a non-trivial tree, no mainnet-fork rehearsal, and no invariant that a freshly-deployed root be non-zero. This is the purest illustration that *organizational* controls (rehearsal, invariant assertions, staged rollout with caps) fail independently of code quality.

**Cross-cutting conclusion.** Key-count, threshold, timelock, and hardware-wallet practice are genuinely governance signals, and they are *necessary but not sufficient*: Radiant satisfied hardware-wallet, geographic-distribution, and multi-review requirements and still lost $50M [B33], because the adversarial entry point was the human endpoint and the review tooling was itself deceived. The additional properties that would have changed outcomes are all organizational: out-of-band confirmation for any high-value transaction, mandatory dual-channel approval for signer-set changes, hardware-backed transaction-decoding (not blind signing), no automatic retry-on-error UX in the signing path, and a simulated full-signing rehearsal before any ownership transfer. SEAL's checklist operationalises these — classify and document each multisig, verify and identify all signers before operations begin, run emergency-notification tests, maintain backup signing infrastructure and *tested alternative UIs*, and verify that all signers completed training [B39].

**Verifiable checks:**
1. Count Safe modules/guards enabled (`getModulesPaginated`, `getGuard`, `getFallbackHandler`). Non-standard guards and enable-modules hooks are a signing-surface expansion; SEAL says no modules/guards without documented justification and review [B9].
2. Verify threshold ≥50% and N≥3 (N≥7 for >$1M) [B9]; reject N-of-N.
3. Confirm each owner is a distinct hardware-wallet address — not an exchange deposit address, not a smart-account with modules, not a shared-seed address.
4. For every Safe, confirm signer-set changes require the timelock (or are themselves a queued proposal), and check the grace period on `changeThreshold` / `swapOwner`.
5. Verify that a documented, tested alternative signing UI/infrastructure exists (single-UI dependency is a liveness and phishing risk).
6. Check whether the protocol's *only* path to a privileged action is a hardware-wallet signature with no on-chain delay.
7. Search incident history for "multisig" / "signer" / "key compromise" — a protocol that has had one is materially differently positioned than one that has not, regardless of current config.

## B7 What makes a token-holder's governance rights *real*: falsifying the claims

### Findings

Every decentralization claim reduces to a small set of falsifiable statements. Each row below is a claim → the single check that kills it.

**"Fully decentralized."** Unpacks into: (i) no single party can change the protocol's behaviour; (ii) no party can extract value from it; (iii) no party can censor it. **Falsifier:** show one address whose single (or threshold) signature can call a state-changing privileged function on any user-fund-holding contract. A 2-of-2 proxy admin [B4] or a 12-member Security Council with genuine chain-owner power over core parameters and code [B7] refutes (i).

**"DAO-owned."** Unpacks into: the DAO (through its Governor → Timelock) is the *only* admin of the protocol. **Falsifier:** read `owner()`/`admin()` on the implementation, the factory, the pause authority, and the treasury; if any is not the timelock, the DAO does not own it. Concretely: OP Stack publishes that the System Config Owner is a 5-of-7 multisig that "may eventually be operated by a Security Council" and "can be replaced by the L1 Proxy Admin" [B4] — three distinct controllers of one parameter set, none of which is a token vote.

**"No admin keys."** This is the claim most often technically false and most often not intended to be false. Uniswap's own v2 whitepaper admits: "While the contract is not generally upgradeable, **there is a private key that has the ability to update a variable on the factory contract to turn on an on-chain 5-basis-point fee on trades.** This fee will initially be turned off, but could be turned on in the future" [B40]. **Falsifier:** grep the factory/proxy for owner-gated setters, fee knobs, pause functions, and upgrade entry points. "Non-upgradeable" ≠ "no admin key" — Uniswap's own documentation is the proof, and it is a *fee-extraction* key, i.e. it has economic value to exercise.

**"Emergency powers are limited."** Unpacks into: emergency actions can pause but cannot upgrade, mint, or move funds, and expire. **Falsifier:** enumerate the reachable function set from the emergency address. OP Stack's Guardian satisfies this narrowly — it can "pause withdrawals," blocking L2→L1 execution [B40]. Arbitrum's Security Council does *not* [B7]. The claim is falsified by one upgradeable proxy in the emergency role's reach.

**"Governed by token holders."** Unpacks into: votes are cast by a broad, non-captured set of token holders. **Falsifier:** Nakamoto coefficient ≤ small single digits and community-delegate share ≤10% [B11][B12]; or voting-HHI > holding-HHI [B14]; or <5% of supply having ever voted.

**"Signers are independent."** Unpacks into: no single employer, funder, or founder controls a threshold. **Falsifier:** map every owner address to its funding source. SushiSwap's 2020 "decentralized" treasury was a **nine-signer multisig requiring any six of nine**, whose elected members included Sam Bankman-Fried (FTX), CMS Holdings (an investment firm), and Matthew Graham (Sino Global Capital) — i.e. an elected board that was substantially a board of other financial interests in the sector [B41]. An *election* does not create independence; the independence question is about whose P&L benefits.

**"Transparent."** Unpacks into: the claim can be checked by a third party without asking the team. **Falsifier:** the treasury number cannot be resolved to on-chain addresses, or the financial statement is not published, or the incident history has no primary post-mortem [B21][B22][B32][B33][B35].

**Scoring rule that follows:** for any "decentralized / DAO-owned / no admin keys / community-owned" claim, the score should be a **count of refutations**, not a binary. Each of the seven falsifiers above is cheap (one `cast call` plus one address-label lookup), deterministic, and refutable by a third party without cooperation. That is what makes them useful scoring signals rather than marketing categories.

**Verifiable checks (the seven falsifiers as a routine):**
1. `owner()`/`admin()` on every contract holding user funds or defining parameters → walk to terminal controller.
2. Owner-gated function enumeration on factories/proxies (fee knobs, pause, upgrade, mint) — the Uniswap v2 case is the template.
3. Nakamoto coefficient + community-delegate share.
4. Voting-HHI ÷ holding-HHI after PCA and exchange exclusions.
5. Emergency-role reachable function set (pause-only vs. upgrade/mint/transfer).
6. Signer→employer/funder mapping, with a threshold-collision check (does any 3 owners share an employer or grant?).
7. Treasury address resolvability + statement of audit status.
